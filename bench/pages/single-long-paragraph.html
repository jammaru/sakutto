A single paragraph about the survey

Methods notebook · 24 October

When the survey closed, the team expected a few hundred replies and received slightly more than four thousand, which was more than enough to make the original plan of reading every answer by hand impossible and more than enough to make the alternative plan of sampling a tidy hundred look suspicious to the people who had written the other three thousand nine hundred, so the team settled on a compromise that satisfied nobody completely: they read the first two hundred answers in the order they arrived, then every answer that contained more than forty words, then a random sample of the remainder drawn with a seed published alongside the dataset so that anyone who doubted the selection could reproduce it exactly. The first thing that became clear was that the ratings and the sentences had almost nothing to do with each other, because a respondent who chose the middle option on every scale could still write a paragraph describing a specific morning in February when the boat had been cancelled twice and the replacement bus had arrived an hour late with no announcement at either end of the route, and a respondent who chose the lowest option on every scale could write that the service was fine except for the price, which was the one thing the review had been told not to touch. The second thing that became clear was that the word reliable appeared in about a fifth of all the free-text answers and was used in at least three different senses, sometimes to mean that a sailing had departed on time, sometimes to mean that a departure had been cancelled early enough that an alternative could be found, and sometimes to mean that the staff at the booking office had answered the telephone and explained what was happening, which suggests that the authority had been measuring punctuality while its passengers had been measuring something closer to predictability and that the two measurements agreed only in the middle of the distribution where nothing interesting happens. Nobody on the team was surprised by the third finding, which was that the answers written in the second and third languages of the survey were systematically longer and more detailed than the answers written in the first, but everybody was surprised by the fourth finding, which was that the shortest answers in the entire dataset came from the respondents who travelled most often and who had the most specific complaints to make, and that those answers were almost always written in the same four or five phrases, as though a daily commuter learns to compress a year of frustration into the smallest possible unit of language that will still be understood by the person reading it at the other end.
