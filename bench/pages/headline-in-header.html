Funding the harbour libraries

Today the cooperative is placing part of its own reserve into a new, separately governed body, the Harbour Library Fund, and is committing to add to it each year that its membership grows. Subject to a vote at the spring meeting, the first transfer will happen this month, and a further two percent of the reserve will follow every time the cooperative passes one of the membership marks it has already published.

Most of what the cooperative does for its mission happens inside the cooperative itself: it runs reading rooms, repairs donated books, trains volunteers and lends equipment to schools along the coast. Those activities have a public side and a practical side, and keeping them in-house lets the members who use them decide how they are run.

The fund will be more flexible and larger than anything the cooperative could run from its own budget, which lets it work in ways the cooperative cannot. It might, for example, make large grants to established literacy charities, or pay for books directly for families who live too far from any branch to borrow them in person.

Why a separate fund

One example is the closure of small branches. Moving lending online may save a great deal of money, but it can also leave older readers and people without a reliable connection with nothing at all. The cooperative has already spent a large share of last year's surplus studying the problem, but at the pace branches are closing, a bolder response may be needed, and the fund is meant to be able to make it.

So that it can give through many different channels, the fund is set up as a company owned by the cooperative rather than as a charity of its own. The shares the fund holds will never be used for lobbying or for donations to a political campaign, in this country or anywhere else, and that rule is written into its articles.

This builds on commitments the founders and staff have already made. When the cooperative was formed, its founders pledged most of their own stake to public causes, and since then it has matched every donation a member makes to a library with one of its own, up to a published yearly limit.

How the fund is governed

The fund has a board of five. Two seats belong to people elected by the members, two belong to librarians nominated by the regional association, and the fifth is held by someone the other four agree on. No member of staff can hold a seat, and no seat can be held for more than two terms in a row.

The board publishes every grant it makes, with the amount, the recipient and a short reason, within a month of the decision. Once a year it also publishes a longer report that says which grants did what they were meant to do and which did not, because a fund that only reports its successes cannot learn from the rest.

  • Grants to libraries that keep a branch open.
  • Books posted to readers who cannot travel.
  • Training for volunteers in remote towns.

What happens next

The first call for proposals opens after the vote. Libraries, schools and reading groups will be able to apply through a short form, and the board has said it will answer every application, including the ones it turns down, with a sentence or two explaining why.

We think this is the right shape for the work: a body that can move money quickly to the places that need it, that answers to readers and librarians rather than to the cooperative's own managers, and that has to explain itself in public every time it spends.
