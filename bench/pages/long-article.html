The long road to a reliable ferry timetable

For eleven months, a small harbor authority rewrote the schedule that six thousand people depend on every day. The arguments over minutes turned out to be arguments about trust.

The first draft of the winter timetable arrived on a Tuesday, stapled into three bundles and left on the counter of the booking office beside a bowl of peppermints. It moved the seven o'clock sailing four minutes earlier and the last crossing eleven minutes later, and nobody who read it believed those numbers were the important part.

A timetable is a promise made in advance by people who understand how rarely the sea keeps promises. The authority had spent the previous season apologising for cancellations, and the new document was an attempt to apologise less often by admitting more about the weather, the tides, and the age of the boats.

How the review began

The review began with a spreadsheet of every crossing made in the previous three years, joined to a log of wind speeds recorded at the outer buoy. Two analysts spent a fortnight matching delays to conditions and found that almost half of the late sailings happened in a narrow band of wind directions that funnelled between the headland and the eastern breakwater.

That finding changed the shape of the conversation. Instead of arguing about whether a particular morning had been unusually rough, the committee could point at a pattern that repeated itself three or four times a month and ask what the schedule should do about it in advance rather than afterwards.

The harbourmaster, who had worked the crossing for nineteen years before taking the desk job, was sceptical of the spreadsheet at first. She pointed out that the buoy measures wind at a height no deck experiences and that a single number cannot describe a sea that arrives from two directions at once.

We do not need a forecast that is always right. We need a promise that is honest enough that people can plan around it.

The tidal window

The eastern pier was built for boats that were shorter than the ones now in service, and at the lowest tides of the month the ramp sits at an angle that makes loading slow. The authority had quietly absorbed the cost of that angle for years by padding the turnaround time, which meant the published schedule was already a generous document nobody trusted.

Padding is a strange kind of honesty. It protects the operator from blame and costs the passenger a few minutes every day, and because the pad is invisible, passengers learn to treat the printed time as an aspiration. One of the review's recommendations was to publish the tide window instead of hiding it inside an unexplained gap.

That recommendation produced the longest argument of the winter. Publishing the window would mean admitting that some crossings cannot be punctual, and the authority's board was worried that a visible constraint would look like an excuse rather than a plan.

What passengers said

The survey went out in three languages and came back with four thousand replies, which for a route of this size was unprecedented. The free-text answers were more useful than the ratings, because they described the situations that made a late boat expensive: a hospital appointment, a shift handover, a school gate that closes at ten past eight.

One respondent wrote that she did not mind waiting when she knew the wait was coming, and that the worst mornings were the ones where the board showed a time that had already passed. Several others described the same thing in different words, and the pattern was impossible to ignore.

The review team began to think of the timetable as two documents: the one printed on the wall and the one the authority actually believed. Closing the distance between them looked like a cheaper improvement than buying a faster boat, and it had the advantage of being possible within a single budget cycle.

Freight, crews, and the night crossing

Freight operators had the least patience for the review and the most to lose from a badly sequenced change. A van that misses the nine o'clock crossing loses a morning of deliveries on the far side, and two of the island's three grocers plan their week around that single sailing.

Crew rest rules set a hard boundary around any discussion of the night crossing. The last boat cannot leave later than it does without shortening the interval before the first morning sailing, and shortening that interval is the one change the union has consistently refused to consider.

So the review worked backwards from the morning. Everything else, including the length of the evening gap, was negotiable as long as the first boat left on time with a full crew and a rested master, which is why the final document looks less like a timetable than a chain of dependencies with a hull attached.

The cost model added a further constraint that nobody had expected. Running the same number of crossings with a wider buffer cost slightly less in fuel, because boats that are not racing a deadline hold a steadier speed, and slightly more in staff hours, because the turnaround allowance grew at both ends of the day.

Island residents made the environmental case in the plainest terms available. The ferry is the clinic's supply line, the school's second bus, and the only way a plumber reaches a broken pump in February, so a schedule that fails quietly in bad weather is a public health problem dressed as a transport problem.

Ticketing changed too, though more slowly than the schedule. The authority replaced its paper punch cards with a scanner that works in the rain, and the first month produced a predictable crop of duplicate charges. The refunds were quick, and the goodwill survived mostly because the office answered the telephone.

By the time the final draft went to the board, the document had grown four appendices and lost two sailings. It also contained a sentence that would have been unthinkable a year earlier: when conditions exceed the stated limits, the crossing will not depart, and the decision will be published within twenty minutes.

The board approved it by a single vote, which the harbourmaster described afterwards as the correct margin for a document that promises less and delivers more. The new schedule takes effect in three weeks, and the first test of it will not be the weather but whether anyone bothers to read the appendices.
