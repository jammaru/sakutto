A small co-op bought its own grid, and the bills went down

Six villages pooled their savings and bought the substation that had been raising their prices for a decade.

The vote took four minutes and had been eight years in the making. On a wet Tuesday in March, sixty-one households agreed to borrow against their own roofs to buy a substation that had changed hands three times since the grid was privatised, and whose last owner had raised the standing charge twice in eighteen months without explaining why.

Nobody in the room was under the illusion that the purchase would be simple. The substation is thirty-one years old, its transformers were last rewound in 2011, and the easement that carries the line across the eastern field expires in 2031 unless the landowner agrees to renew it. The co-op's solicitor read all three problems aloud before the vote, which is the kind of thing that usually kills a proposal.

What carried it instead was arithmetic on a single sheet of paper. The standing charge alone accounted for a little over a fifth of what a typical household paid each year, and the co-op's model showed that owning the asset and paying a contractor to maintain it would cost less than that within four years, even with a pessimistic assumption about how often the transformers would fail.

The numbers behind the vote

The model was built by a retired distribution engineer who had spent twenty-two years in the regional utility and who described his spreadsheet, without irony, as the least interesting document he had ever produced. It projects maintenance costs, replacement costs, insurance and the interest on the loan, then compares the total against the charges the co-op would otherwise have paid.

The interesting column is the one labelled "failure", which carries an estimate of how many times a year the older transformer will trip in cold weather. The engineer set that number at four, which is twice the rate the utility had recorded, because he did not trust the utility's records. Even at four, the co-op still comes out ahead by the fourth winter.

We are not trying to beat the grid. We are trying to stop paying for the parts of it that never reach us.

That line, from the co-op's chair, became the slogan on the leaflet that went to every household. It is also, more or less, the legal theory behind the purchase: the co-op is not building a rival network, it is acquiring the last mile that it already pays to use, and hiring professionals to run it on the same standards the regulator requires.

The regulator's response has been cautious but not hostile. Its published guidance allows a community to own distribution assets, provided the operation is licensed, the safety obligations are met, and the co-op does not try to serve customers outside its own boundary. The last condition is the awkward one, because two farms on the far side of the line have asked whether they can join.

What the first winter showed

The first winter produced eleven faults, three of them inside a fortnight, all of them on the section of overhead line that runs along the ridge. The contractor was on site within four hours each time, which was faster than the previous owner had managed, and the co-op's outage log is now public, which is new.

Publishing the log changed the tone of the local meetings. Instead of arguing about whether the power had gone off, residents could point at a date and a duration, and the board could answer with a work order. Two of the eleven faults turned out to be caused by a single damaged insulator that had been missed in the handover survey.

The ridge line is now scheduled for replacement in the spring, at a cost the co-op had not budgeted for, funded by a small increase in the monthly contribution that the board approved without a vote because it fell inside the reserve it had set aside. Three households wrote to object, and the board published the letters alongside its reply.

There is a broader lesson in the letters, which is that a co-op's members are not customers and do not behave like them. A customer who is unhappy leaves, and the operator loses revenue. A member who is unhappy turns up at the next meeting and asks detailed questions about the reserve, which is more work and considerably more useful.

The part nobody modelled

What the spreadsheet could not capture was the time. Running a licensed distribution asset is a second job for the four people on the board, and the paperwork is not trivial: safety inspections, insurance renewals, a quarterly return that the regulator reads in a way nobody has yet been able to explain to a volunteer.

The co-op has since hired a part-time clerk, which appears in the model as a cost the engineer had omitted because he assumed the board would absorb it. It is the single largest correction to the original arithmetic, and it pushes the payback out by roughly seven months.

Even so, the board is ahead of its own projection at the eighteen-month mark, mostly because the price of the wholesale power it buys has fallen faster than the model assumed. That is luck rather than judgement, and the chair says so every time the subject comes up at a meeting.

The two farms across the line are still waiting. Serving them would mean extending the licensed boundary, which is a decision for the regulator and not for the co-op, and which the co-op has decided not to pursue until the ridge line has been replaced and a full year has passed without a fault inside the boundary.

In the meantime the substation has a new fence, a new sign, and a small brass plate by the gate that lists the names of the sixty-one households that voted for the purchase. It is the only piece of infrastructure in the valley with a list of owners on the front, and the chair admits she had it made before the vote, in the hope that she would need it.
